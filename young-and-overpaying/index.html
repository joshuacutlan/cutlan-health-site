Guide · Under 35 and insured through work

Young, healthy, and overpaying for your work health insurance?

Most people never look at the health line on their paystub. If you're healthy and under 35, that line is often paying for your coworkers, not you. Five minutes with a paystub tells you.

By Josh Cutlan · independent agent, NPN 22078996
Updated October 21, 2026 · 7-min read
In short

Work plans charge a young, healthy employee the same as everyone else on the plan. If your employer pays most of the bill for you alone, you're probably fine. If a big chunk comes out of your check, or you're paying to add a spouse, a private plan priced on your age and health may come in lower. You'd give up your employer's share and the pre-tax deduction, so compare the real numbers before you switch anything.

You're paying the group price, not your price

An employer health plan is priced for the whole group. The insurer looks at everyone on the plan, young and old, healthy and not, and sets one price. Most employers then split that price the same way for every employee on a tier: everyone with "employee only" coverage pays the same paycheck deduction, and everyone with "employee + spouse" pays the same, and so on.

That's a great deal if you're 58 with a bad knee. If you're 25 and haven't seen a doctor since your last sports physical, you're helping pay for everyone else. Nobody tells you that at orientation. You just see a number on your paystub and assume it's the price of insurance.

Insurance you buy on your own works differently. Price follows age. Under federal rules for individual plans, premiums can be up to 3 times higher for older people than for younger ones (HealthCare.gov). Private plans go a step further and also look at your health. So the younger and healthier you are, the more an individual plan's price tends to work in your favor.

What workers pay on average (and when you're fine)

KFF's 2025 survey of employer plans found the average yearly premium was $9,325 for single coverage and $26,993 for family coverage. Workers paid, on average, $1,440 a year toward single coverage and $6,850 toward family coverage. Employers paid the rest.

Two things jump out:

  • For single coverage, many employers pay most of the bill. If your share is close to that average, about $120 a month, your work plan is likely hard to beat, and you'll hear that from me first.
  • Adding people costs a lot more. The average worker pays nearly five times as much for family coverage as for single. At small firms it's even higher: workers paid an average of $8,889 a year for family coverage, versus $6,227 at large firms.

Averages hide a lot. Some employers pay almost everything. Others, often smaller businesses, pass a big share to employees, and some high-deductible plans come with a deductible a healthy young person would almost never reach. That's where the overpaying usually happens.

The spouse problem nobody explains at onboarding

This is the one I see most with young couples. Your employer may pay a big part of your own coverage, but contribute much less, or nothing, for your wife or husband. Adding them can mean paying close to full price for their coverage through payroll, at the same group rate as a 55-year-old coworker's spouse.

If both of you are young and healthy, a separate private plan for your spouse, or for both of you, can sometimes cost less. Since 2023, whether family coverage is affordable is based on what you'd pay to cover the family, not just yourself, so depending on your income, your spouse might qualify for help there even if you don't. Either way, it's worth comparing before you sign up for the "employee + spouse" tier again.

Signs you're probably overpaying

You're a good candidate to compare if most of these sound like you:

  • You're roughly 20 to 35 and in good health.
  • You rarely go to the doctor and don't take ongoing prescriptions.
  • A big amount comes out of each paycheck, or you're paying to cover a spouse.
  • Your work plan has a high deductible you've never come close to meeting.
  • You want to pick your own specialists without a referral.

When the right call is keeping your work plan

Part of the job is saying so when the plan you have is the one to keep. Keep your employer plan if:

  • Your employer pays most of it. If your share for single coverage is small, it's very hard to beat.
  • A health condition or regular medications. A private plan can say no or cover everything except that condition. Employer plans can't.
  • You're planning a baby soon. Check maternity coverage closely. Many private plans cap it or leave it out entirely.
  • You'd lose something that matters, like an HSA contribution from your employer, or a plan that lists your family's doctors.

The catches, spelled out

You give up your employer's share

The money your employer puts toward your premium is part of your pay. Most employers won't hand it to you if you skip the plan, so the comparison is your paycheck deduction versus the full price of a private plan.

You lose the pre-tax break

Most work plans take your share out before taxes through what's called a cafeteria plan (IRS Publication 15-B). A private plan is usually paid with after-tax money. If you're in a 22% tax bracket, $200 of pre-tax premium "costs" you about $140 to $156 out of pocket, depending on whether it also skips payroll taxes (Social Security and Medicare). I build that into the comparison. Check the tax side with your tax preparer; this isn't tax advice.

Timing is locked

Payroll elections are normally fixed for the plan year. Most people can only drop their work plan at their employer's open enrollment, often in the fall, or once a qualifying life event comes along. Private plans can usually start the first of any month, so if you're approved, the start date gets set so there isn't a single uninsured day.

Private plans are priced for you, not the whole office

A private plan prices you on your age and health instead of averaging you in with everyone at work. That's why it can cost less for a healthy 25-year-old, and it's why we go over what a plan covers on a screen share before you apply.

Marketplace savings usually aren't available to you

If your job offers coverage that's affordable and meets minimum standards, you generally can't get Marketplace savings for yourself (HealthCare.gov). Most job-based plans meet those standards.

How I run the numbers

  1. Find your real cost. Grab a recent paystub. Multiply your health deduction by your number of paychecks. $150 every two weeks is $3,900 a year.
  2. Look at what you used. Doctor visits, prescriptions, anything coming up. Your plan's deductible and out-of-pocket max matter more than the premium if you use care.
  3. Quote private options for your age, state, and health, with the same or better doctor access.
  4. Compare after taxes, including what you'd give up, and pick the switch date around your employer's open enrollment.

If your work plan comes out ahead, you've lost nothing but a few minutes. If it doesn't, the savings can add up to real money every year.

Where I land

Being healthy and under 35 is the one time the individual market is priced in your favor. Work plans don't care. If your employer pays most of your own premium, keep it and don't think twice. If a big chunk leaves every paycheck, or you're covering a spouse, one comparison tells you. Do it before your employer's open enrollment closes, because that's usually your only chance this year.

What people ask me

Can I drop my employer's health insurance whenever I want?+

Usually not. Paycheck deductions for health insurance are normally locked in for the plan year, so most people can only drop at their employer's open enrollment, or once a qualifying life event such as marriage or a new baby comes along. Ask your HR team for your plan's rules before you change anything.

Is a private plan as good as my employer plan?+

Plan by plan, it varies. A private plan can come with a wide PPO network, but its coverage list is its own, so we read it together before you decide.

Can I get a Marketplace subsidy instead of my work plan?+

Probably not for yourself. If your employer offers coverage that's affordable and meets minimum standards, you usually can't get Marketplace savings for you. Since 2023, family coverage is judged on what it costs to cover the family, so a spouse may qualify. If the Marketplace is the better route for your spouse, that's what I'll recommend.

Does my employer have to pay me more if I drop their plan?+

No. Most employers don't pay you their share if you skip the plan. That money is part of what you give up, which is why I compare total cost, not just premiums.

Is it free to have you compare?+

Yes. The carrier pays my commission, you never get a bill from me, and you're never obligated to switch.
