Health insurance for truck drivers: the plan has to cover where you run

Nobody hands an owner-operator a health plan, and the plan most drivers end up with was built for someone who sleeps at home every night. Here's how to buy one that holds up between states, what a DOT physical does and doesn't have to do with it, and why I take this one personally.

By Josh Cutlan · independent agent, NPN 22078996
Updated October 22, 2026 · 9-min read
In short

Most owner-operators and lease drivers are 1099, so no company plan exists. You buy where you live, but you have to pick for where you run: many Marketplace plans are local HMOs or EPOs that cover nothing outside the network except an emergency, so for a driver who sleeps in three states a week, a PPO is usually the first thing to price. A DOT physical is a work exam, not free preventive care, and the conditions that can shorten your medical card are exactly what a real health plan pays to manage.

Why I take this one personally

My grandfather owned a trucking and transportation company, so I grew up around truckers, in my family and around it. I never understood the insurance side until I started this career. Now I see the same problem over and over: a plan whose network stops at the state line, bought by someone who's in a different state every day. For a trucker the first question is whether it's a PPO that covers you wherever you run, before price, deductible or anything else. That's why this page exists.

Who this is for

  • Owner-operators and lease-purchase drivers. You're 1099, and nobody is enrolling you in anything.
  • Company drivers paying the family rate. Your plan is priced for the whole fleet, not for you.
  • Regional, over-the-road, hotshot and car haulers. Anyone whose week crosses a state line.
  • Drivers leaving a fleet job to run their own authority. The benefits end with the job, and the clock to replace them runs 60 days.

The network has to cover where you run

Health plans sell by home address: your zip sets the price and the doctor list. Most Marketplace plans run on an HMO or an EPO, and HealthCare.gov's description of both comes down to the same sentence: outside the network, nothing is covered except an emergency. A PPO is the type that pays toward doctors outside its network, at a higher share, and doesn't make you get a referral first.

For someone who lives and works in one county, that's a fair trade for a lower premium. For a driver, it means the strep throat in Amarillo, the back spasm in Ohio and the refill you need in Georgia are all out of network. An emergency is the one exception: a true emergency is covered at any hospital, and the plan can't charge you more for an out-of-network ER or make you call first (HealthCare.gov). Everything short of an emergency is where drivers get burned.

So the first question I ask a trucker isn't about budget. It's "Where do you sleep?" Then I look for a PPO whose network reaches those states, or, if your health or income points to the Marketplace, the Marketplace plan in your state with the widest network I can find.

Buy for home, pick for the road

You buy a plan in the state where you live, priced for your zip, even if you're home four days a month. Move your home base to another state and the old plan doesn't come along; HealthCare.gov is blunt that a move out of state means a new application and a new plan. I'm licensed in 19 states, and the list covers most of trucking's home turf: Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin, Missouri, Kansas and Kentucky in the Midwest, plus Texas, Oklahoma, Tennessee, the Carolinas, Georgia, Virginia, Maryland, Colorado, Utah and Florida.

The DOT physical, and what insurance does and doesn't do

Every CDL holder knows the drill: a medical examiner on FMCSA's National Registry signs your Medical Examiner's Certificate, good for up to 24 months, and can issue a shorter one to keep an eye on a condition like high blood pressure (FMCSA). Two things trip people up.

First, the exam itself usually isn't "free with insurance." It's a work requirement, not one of the preventive services every Marketplace plan has to cover at no cost. That list is blood pressure checks, cholesterol, vaccines, cancer screenings and the like, with no employment or driver exam on it (HealthCare.gov). Most drivers pay a flat clinic fee; one national walk-in chain lists $150, takes HSA cards and doesn't bill insurance for it. Some plans do pay toward the exam, so it's one of the questions I ask about any plan you're considering.

Second, and this is the one that matters: the things that can shrink or cost you your card are medical. Blood pressure. Blood sugar. The sleep study an examiner asks for. Those are doctor visits, prescriptions and tests, and paying for them, after your deductible, is what a health plan is for. A driver with no coverage puts off the visit, the numbers drift, and the next physical comes back as a short card instead of two years. Coverage protects the card.

A Marketplace plan

Takes every applicant and covers what you already have. With a premium tax credit in play it can cost less than anything else, and for a driver with a condition a private plan won't take, it's the answer. The catch is the network: check that the plan covers more than your home county before you fall for the premium. Above the credit's ceiling, you're paying full price for a local network, and that's when a private PPO deserves the first quote.

A private PPO

Priced on your age and health, often with a network that reaches across many states, and it can usually start the first of any month with no enrollment window. The coverage list is the plan's own, so we go through it together on a screen share before you apply. For a healthy driver above the credit's ceiling, it's usually the one to price first.

A spouse's plan

If your spouse has a W-2 job, price getting added. Sometimes the employer pays enough that it beats everything. Sometimes the spouse tier is the most expensive line on the page, and a private plan for you alone wins.

The traps at the truck stop

  • An occupational accident policy is not health insurance. It pays for injuries on the job. It does nothing for the flu, the blood pressure, or the gallbladder.
  • Things sold as "coverage" that aren't a health plan. Discount cards, sharing programs, fixed-dollar plans that pay a few hundred dollars toward a hospital day that costs thousands. Ask three questions: is there an out-of-pocket maximum, does it cover a hospital stay, and is there a network list. A no on any of them means it isn't the plan you think it is.
  • Skipping it because you feel fine. One rollover, one night in an out-of-state ER with nothing behind you, and the truck payment is the least of it.
  • Picking on premium alone. Check the deductible, the out-of-pocket max and the network map, in that order.

The tax side

If you show a profit and don't have access to an employer-subsidized plan, including through a spouse, the IRS may let you deduct your premiums using Form 7206, the self-employed health insurance deduction. Ask your tax preparer; this isn't tax advice. If you get a Marketplace credit, keep your income estimate current: a strong year of loads can mean part of it comes due when you file.

What people ask me

Does health insurance pay for a DOT physical?+

Usually not as a free visit. The exam that keeps your medical card is a work requirement, and it isn't on the list of preventive services every Marketplace plan must cover at no cost. Walk-in clinics tend to charge a flat self-pay fee, often around $150. What a health plan does cover is the care behind the card: the blood pressure visits, prescriptions and tests an examiner may ask for. Some plans will pay toward the exam itself, so I ask before you buy.

I'm an owner-operator. Where do I even buy a plan?+

In the state where you live, not where you run. Marketplace plans are sold by home address and zip, and so are private plans. Move your home base to a new state and you need a new plan. I'm licensed in 19 states, including Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin, Missouri, Kansas, Kentucky and Texas, so for most drivers I can price plans for home.

Can an HMO or EPO work for a long-haul driver?+

Only if you're home when you need care. HealthCare.gov describes both types the same way: outside the network, nothing is covered except an emergency. A true emergency is covered at any hospital, and the plan can't charge you more for an out-of-network ER. The problem is everything that isn't an emergency: an urgent care two states over, a follow-up visit, a refill 800 miles from home.

Will a private PPO take me if I have high blood pressure or sleep apnea?+

Sometimes. Each carrier judges each condition differently. Private plans ask health questions and can say no, or cover everything except that condition. If a private plan won't work, a Marketplace plan takes everyone, and some states offer Marketplace plans whose networks reach further. I check both before you apply to anything, so a decline never lands on your record by surprise.

Can I deduct my premiums as a 1099 driver?+

Often, yes. With a net profit and no access to an employer-subsidized plan, including through a spouse, the IRS may let you deduct what you pay for health insurance using Form 7206. Run it past your tax preparer; this isn't tax advice.

I drive for a company with a plan, but the family rate is brutal. Options?+

Compare it. A company plan charges every driver the same family rate, whatever your age or health. If you and your spouse are healthy, a private plan for the family, or for everyone but you, priced on age and health can come in lower. And since 2023, whether family coverage counts as affordable is judged on what it costs to cover the family, so your spouse may qualify for Marketplace savings even when you don't.
