Premium vs deductible: what you pay for health insurance

Most people pick the plan with the lowest monthly price and find out what a deductible is the first time they need care. Here's what each number means, and a simple way to see which plan costs you less in a good year and a bad one.

By Josh Cutlan · independent agent, NPN 22078996
Updated October 21, 2026 · 5-min read
In short

The premium is the fixed monthly charge that keeps the policy active, used or not. The deductible is the slice of your own covered medical bills you cover first each year. After that you usually pay coinsurance (a percentage) or copays until you reach the out-of-pocket maximum, the yearly ceiling on what covered care can cost you. To compare plans, add up a year of premiums, then picture a year where you barely see a doctor and a year where you hit the ceiling.

The four numbers, in the order you pay them

NumberWhat it isWhen you pay it
PremiumThe price of having the planEvery month, even if you never use it
DeductibleThe slice of your covered bills you cover first each yearWhen you get care, until you've paid this much in the year
Coinsurance / copayYour share after the deductible (say 20%), or a set dollar amount per visitAfter the deductible, or for some visits before it
Out-of-pocket maxThe yearly ceiling on what covered care can cost youReach it, and covered care costs you nothing more that year

One thing trips people up: premiums don't count toward the deductible or the out-of-pocket max. Those two track only the money you spend on care.

Why the lowest monthly price can cost you more

Plans trade these numbers against each other. Lower the monthly premium and the deductible usually goes up. That trade works if you barely see a doctor and could absorb a big bill. It's a problem if a $7,000 surprise would wreck you.

So the useful question isn't "which plan has the lowest monthly price?" It's "what would each plan cost me in a quiet year, and in my worst year?"

Two plans, a good year and a bad year

A made-up example with round numbers. A 27-year-old is choosing between two plans:

Low-premium planLow-deductible plan
Premium$280 a month ($3,360 a year)$410 a month ($4,920 a year)
Deductible$7,500$1,500
Out-of-pocket max$9,000$5,000
Quiet year (a checkup, nothing else)$3,360$4,920
Worst year (hits the max)$12,360$9,920

In a quiet year, the low-premium plan comes out ahead by $1,560. In a year where both plans hit their max, the low-deductible plan comes out ahead by $2,440. Neither is "the better plan." Which one is right depends on how likely the bad year is for you, and whether you could pay $12,360 if it came.

Do this math for any plans you're comparing: premiums for the year, plus $0 for a quiet year, plus the out-of-pocket max for the worst year.

What happens in a bad year

Say you break your wrist and the covered bills come to $12,000. On a plan with a $3,000 deductible, 20% coinsurance and a $6,000 out-of-pocket max:

  1. You pay the first $3,000. That's the deductible.
  2. You pay 20% of the remaining $9,000, which is $1,800.
  3. Your total is $4,800, under the $6,000 max, so the plan pays the other $7,200.

Run up more bills that year and you'd stop paying at $6,000. That cap is the number that protects you, which is why I look at it before the premium.

Marketplace caps vs private plans

Marketplace plans have to stay under a federal out-of-pocket limit. For 2027, HealthCare.gov lists it at $12,000 per person and $24,000 per family. Many plans set theirs lower.

Private plans don't have to follow that limit, and they can be built differently: some cap your costs per year, some pay set amounts per service. Private plans also ask health questions and can say no. Before you pick one, find its own maximum and what counts toward it. I go through that line by line.

Four mistakes I see with young people

  • Picking on premium alone. The monthly price is only part of what you pay.
  • Not knowing the out-of-pocket max. It's the worst-case number. Know it before you need it.
  • Assuming every visit hits the deductible. Preventive care is free on Marketplace plans, and many plans have copays for visits before the deductible.
  • Forgetting the network. Care outside the network may not count toward your deductible at all.

How I compare plans

  1. List each plan's yearly premium, its deductible, and its out-of-pocket max.
  2. Price a quiet year and a worst year for each.
  3. Check what's covered before the deductible: visits, prescriptions, urgent care.
  4. Check that your doctors and nearby hospitals are in network.
  5. Pick the plan whose worst year you could handle, then the one that costs least among those.

Where I land

The premium is the price of having a plan. The deductible and out-of-pocket max are what using it costs. If you're healthy and could cover the out-of-pocket max in a bad year, a lower premium usually wins. If you couldn't, pay more each month for a lower deductible and a lower ceiling. What people ask me

What's a premium, and what's a deductible?+

The premium is the fixed monthly charge that keeps the policy active, used or not. The deductible is the slice of your own covered medical bills you cover first each year before the insurer pays its share. Cheap months usually mean an expensive deductible, and the reverse.

Do monthly payments chip away at the deductible?+

No. Premiums buy you the plan itself. The deductible and the out-of-pocket maximum count only your own spending on care the plan covers.

What is the out-of-pocket maximum?+

The yearly ceiling on what covered care can cost you. Deductibles, coinsurance and copays count toward it; premiums don't. For 2027, HealthCare.gov lists the Marketplace limit at $12,000 per person and $24,000 per family.

Do I pay the deductible for a checkup?+

On Marketplace plans, certain preventive care is covered in full even before you meet your deductible. Many plans also cover some visits with a flat copay before the deductible. Check the plan's summary of benefits.

Is a high-deductible plan a bad idea if I'm young?+

Not necessarily. If you're healthy and could absorb a worst-year bill up to the out-of-pocket maximum, a lower premium can save money most years. If a big bill would sink you, a lower deductible may be worth the higher premium.
