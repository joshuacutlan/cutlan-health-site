What happens to your health insurance subsidy if your income goes up?

A good year can come with a bill in April. If you get a Marketplace subsidy and end up earning more than you estimated, you may have to pay some or all of it back. Here's how that works, where the cliff is for 2027, and what to do before tax time.

By Josh Cutlan, licensed health insurance agent · NPN 22078996
Updated October 14, 2026 · 6-min read
Quick answer

Your Marketplace subsidy is based on what you expect to earn. When you file taxes, it's checked against what you actually earned, and if you got too much, you pay the difference back. If your income ends up above 400% of the federal poverty level (about $63,840 for one person shopping for 2027), you lose the credit for the year and repay all of it. Update your estimate as soon as your income changes, and if you're over the line every year, compare a private plan.

Your subsidy is a guess that gets graded in April

When you sign up on the Marketplace, you estimate your income for the year. That estimate sets your premium tax credit, and most people take it in advance so it lowers the monthly bill. The final check happens at tax time.

Then you file Form 8962 with your return. It compares the credit you received with the credit you qualified for based on your real income. Earned less than you thought? You may get money back. Earned more? You repay the difference, and starting in 2026 there's no cap on how much. HealthCare.gov puts it plainly: if you don't report the change, you'll have to pay back the difference.

For W-2 employees with a steady paycheck, this rarely bites. For self-employed and 1099 workers, it's one of the most common surprises at tax time, because nobody knows in November what next year's contracts will look like.

The cliff: where "some back" becomes "all of it back"

The premium tax credit has a ceiling again. Starting with tax year 2026, the IRS says you can't claim it if your household income is more than 400% of the federal poverty line, and you have to repay all of the advance payments you received.

Here's what that line looks like for 2027 coverage, which uses the 2026 poverty guideline of $15,960 for one person:

The cliff is what makes this different from a normal tax bracket. One dollar under the line, you keep your credit. A few dollars over, you lose the whole year's credit. The Marketplace counts modified adjusted gross income, so it's your income after certain deductions, not the number on your invoices.

What it looks like in real life

Here's a made-up example to show the math. A 28-year-old freelance designer signs up in December and estimates $52,000 for the next year. At that income, she gets a credit that knocks $250 a month off her premium, $3,000 for the year.

In June, she lands a retainer. She finishes the year at $68,000 and doesn't update her estimate. When she files, her income is over 400% of the poverty line for one person. She doesn't qualify for any credit, so the full $3,000 comes due with her return, on top of the taxes on the extra income.

If she had updated her estimate in June, the Marketplace would have cut her credit for the rest of the year. Her monthly bill goes up, and her April bill shrinks to the credit she already got from January through May. It doesn't go away: above the line, she repays every advance payment. If she'd known she'd stay above the line, she could have compared a private plan that doesn't depend on income at all.

Four moves when your income is climbing

1. Update your estimate the month it changes

Log in to your Marketplace account and change your income estimate. The Marketplace recalculates your credit for the rest of the year. The sooner you do it, the fewer months are paid at the wrong amount.

2. Take less of the credit up front

You don't have to take the whole credit in advance. You can choose to apply less of it to your monthly premium and claim the rest when you file. If your income swings, that leaves room before you owe anything back.

3. Ask your tax preparer what lowers the number that counts

The Marketplace uses modified adjusted gross income. Some things that lower your adjusted gross income, like contributing to an HSA if you have an HSA-eligible plan, or certain retirement accounts for the self-employed, can lower it too. Whether that works for you is a question for your tax preparer, especially near the cliff. This isn't tax advice.

4. If you're over the line every year, stop planning around a subsidy

If you're consistently above 400%, the Marketplace is full price for you. That's exactly when a healthy person in their 20s or 30s should compare a private plan priced on age and health. Private plans ask health questions and can say no, may exclude pre-existing conditions, and don't have to cover every ACA benefit, so a Marketplace plan can still be the right call. But you should compare.

Who should stay on the Marketplace anyway

  • Your income comfortably qualifies for help. A real subsidy is hard to beat. Keep it and keep your estimate current.
  • You have a health condition or a baby on the way. Marketplace plans take everyone and cover pre-existing conditions and maternity.
  • Your income is right at the line and could go either way. Stay flexible, take less of the credit up front, and talk to your tax preparer.

Where I land

A subsidy is based on a guess, and the guess gets checked at tax time. If your income goes up, update your Marketplace estimate right away. If you end up over 400% of the poverty line, you repay the whole credit, so near the line, be careful and get tax advice. And if you're over the line every year and healthy, compare a private plan instead of paying full Marketplace price by default.

Questions I get

Do I have to pay back my subsidy if I earn more?+

You may. Your monthly subsidy (the advance premium tax credit) is based on the income you estimated. When you file your taxes, it's compared with what you earned. If you got more than you qualified for, you pay back the difference with your tax return.

What is the subsidy cliff?+

The premium tax credit only goes up to 400% of the federal poverty level. Starting with tax year 2026, the IRS says that if your household income is more than 400% on your tax return, you can't claim the credit and have to repay all of the advance payments. For one person shopping for 2027, that line is about $63,840.

How do I report an income change?+

Log in to your Marketplace account, update your income estimate, and the Marketplace recalculates your savings for the rest of the year. Do it as soon as you know, so fewer months are paid at the wrong amount.

Is there anything that lowers the income the Marketplace counts?+

The Marketplace uses modified adjusted gross income, so some contributions that lower your adjusted gross income, like HSA or certain retirement contributions, can lower it too. That's a question for your tax preparer, not something to guess at. This isn't tax advice.

If I'm over the cliff, what are my options?+

Pay full price for a Marketplace plan, get a plan through a job if you have access to one, or, if you're healthy, compare a private medically underwritten plan priced on your age and health. Private plans ask health questions and can say no, so they don't fit everyone.
