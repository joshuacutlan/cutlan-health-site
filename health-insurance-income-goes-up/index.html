Income went up midyear? Here's what happens to your subsidy

A good year can come with a bill in April. If you get a Marketplace subsidy and end the year above your estimate, you may have to pay some or all of it back. Here's how that works, where the cliff is for 2027, and what to do before tax time.

By Josh Cutlan · independent agent, NPN 22078996
Updated October 21, 2026 · 7-min read
In short

Your Marketplace subsidy is sized to the income you think you'll earn. When you file taxes, it's checked against what you ended up earning, and if you got too much, you pay the difference back. End the year above 400% of the federal poverty level (one person shopping for 2027: about $63,840) and the credit disappears for that year, every advance dollar included. Update your estimate as soon as your income changes, and if you're over the line every year, compare a private plan.

Your subsidy is a guess that gets graded in April

When you sign up on the Marketplace, you estimate what you'll earn over the year. That estimate sets your premium tax credit, and most people take it in advance so it lowers the monthly bill. The final check happens at tax time.

Then you file Form 8962 with your return. It lines up the credit you received against the credit your final income allowed. Earned less than you thought? You may get money back. Earned more? The difference comes back out of your return, and starting in 2026 there's no cap on how much. HealthCare.gov's own warning is blunt: skip reporting the change and you'll have to pay back the difference.

For W-2 employees with a steady paycheck, this rarely bites. For 1099 and self-employed workers, it's among the most common surprises at tax time, because nobody knows in November what next year's contracts will look like.

The cliff: where "some back" becomes "all of it back"

The premium tax credit has a ceiling again. Starting with tax year 2026, the IRS says you can't claim it if your household income is more than 400% of the federal poverty line, and you have to repay all of the advance payments you received.

Here's what that line looks like for 2027 coverage, which uses the 2026 poverty guideline of $15,960 for one person:

The cliff is what makes this different from a normal tax bracket. Finish the year even slightly under the line and every dollar of credit is yours. Finish slightly over and every dollar comes back. The Marketplace counts modified adjusted gross income, so it's your income after certain deductions, not the number on your invoices.

An example with numbers

Here's a made-up example to show the math. A 28-year-old freelance designer signs up in December and estimates $52,000 for the next year. At that income, she gets a credit that knocks $250 a month off her premium, $3,000 for the year.

In June, she lands a retainer. She finishes the year at $68,000 and doesn't update her estimate. When she files, her income is over 400% of the poverty line for one person. She doesn't qualify for any credit, so the full $3,000 comes due with her return, on top of the taxes on the extra income.

If she had updated her estimate in June, the Marketplace would have cut her credit for the rest of the year. Her monthly bill goes up, and her April bill shrinks to the credit she already got from January through May. It doesn't go away: above the line, she repays every advance payment. If she'd known she'd stay above the line, she could have compared a private plan that doesn't depend on income at all.

Four moves when your income is climbing

1. Update your estimate the month it changes

Open your Marketplace account, choose "Report a life change," and enter the new income figure. The Marketplace recalculates your credit for the rest of the year. The sooner you do it, the fewer months run at the wrong amount.

2. Take less of the credit up front

You don't have to take the whole credit in advance. You can choose to apply less of it to your monthly premium and claim the rest when you file. If your income swings, that leaves room before you owe anything back.

3. Ask your tax preparer what pulls the counted number down

The Marketplace counts modified adjusted gross income. Things that lower your adjusted gross income, like contributing to an HSA if you have an HSA-eligible plan, or certain retirement accounts for the self-employed, can pull it down too. Whether that works for you belongs with your tax preparer, especially near the cliff. This isn't tax advice.

4. If you're over the line every year, stop planning around a subsidy

If you're consistently above 400%, the Marketplace charges you its full premium. That's exactly when a healthy person in their 20s or 30s should compare a private plan priced on age and health. Private plans ask health questions and can say no, so a Marketplace plan can still be the right call. But you should compare.

Who should stay on the Marketplace anyway

  • Your income comfortably qualifies for help. A real subsidy is hard to beat. Keep it and keep your estimate current.
  • A health condition, or a baby on the way. Marketplace plans take every applicant and cover ongoing conditions and maternity.
  • Your income sits near the line and could land on either side. Stay flexible, take less of the credit up front, and get your tax preparer involved.

What people ask me

Will I owe my subsidy back if I earn more?+

You may. The monthly subsidy (the advance premium tax credit) is sized to the income you estimated, and your tax return checks it against what you earned. Took more credit than your final income allowed? The gap gets added to what you owe.

What is the subsidy cliff?+

The premium tax credit only goes up to 400% of the federal poverty level. Starting with tax year 2026, the IRS says that if your household income is more than 400% on your tax return, you can't claim the credit and have to repay all of the advance payments. For one person shopping for 2027, that line sits near $63,840.

How do I report an income change?+

Open your Marketplace account, choose "Report a life change," and enter the new income figure. The Marketplace recalculates your savings for the rest of the year. Do it the month it happens, so fewer months run at the wrong amount.

Is there anything that lowers the income the Marketplace counts?+

The number the Marketplace counts is modified adjusted gross income, so contributions that lower your adjusted gross income, like an HSA or certain retirement accounts, can pull it down too. Take that to your tax preparer rather than guessing. This isn't tax advice.

If I'm over the cliff, what are my options?+

Pay the Marketplace plan's full premium, join a job plan if your employer has one, or, if you're healthy, price a private plan built around your age and health. Not everyone is approved for a private plan, which is why I price both.
