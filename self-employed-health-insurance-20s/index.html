Self-employed in your 20s or 30s? Here's how health insurance works for you

Going out on your own is when most people realize nobody is handing them a health plan anymore. Here are your real options, which one usually wins, and the tax break most 1099 workers never claim.

By Josh Cutlan, licensed health insurance agent · NPN 22078996
Updated October 10, 2026
The short answer

You have three main options: stay on a parent's plan until 26, buy a Marketplace plan, or buy a private plan priced on your age and health. If your income is low enough for a Marketplace subsidy, that's often hard to beat. If you earn too much for help and you're healthy, a private PPO is often worth comparing. And if you show a profit, your premiums may be tax deductible.

1. A parent's plan (until 26)

If you're under 26, you can usually stay on a parent's plan even if you're self-employed, married, or out of school. On a parent's Marketplace plan, coverage runs through December 31 of the year you turn 26. On a parent's job-based plan, it generally lasts until 26, but each plan sets its own end date, so ask.

It's often the cheapest option for you, but not always for your parent. Adding you can bump their cost up, and you may not have the doctors you want where you live.

2. A Marketplace plan

Marketplace plans accept everyone and cover pre-existing conditions. What you pay depends on your age, where you live, and your income. If your income qualifies for a premium tax credit, a Marketplace plan can be very affordable. If you earn more, you may get little or no help, and you'd pay full price.

You can enroll during open enrollment (for 2027 coverage, it opens November 1, 2026) or within a special enrollment window after a life event, like losing other coverage. Many Marketplace plans are HMOs or EPOs with local networks.

3. A private plan

Private medically underwritten plans ask health questions and price you on your age and health. For a healthy 20- or 30-something, that often works in your favor, and many offer PPO networks so you can see doctors without referrals. They can usually start in any month.

The trade-offs are real: they can decline an application, exclude pre-existing conditions, and don't have to cover every ACA essential health benefit. I go through exactly what's covered before you apply.

Turning 26 soon? Don't let the birthday sneak up on you

  1. Find your end date. Ask your parent's plan when your coverage stops. It might be your birthday, the end of that month, or the end of the year.
  2. Mark your window. Losing coverage usually opens a special enrollment period for the Marketplace. Private plans don't need one, so you can line up a start date for the day after your old plan ends.
  3. Compare both before you pick. Your income decides whether the Marketplace is the deal or a private plan is.

The mistake I see most: waiting until the coverage has already ended. A gap of even a few weeks is a risk you don't need to take.

The tax break most 1099 workers leave on the table

If you have a net profit from your business and you're not eligible for an employer-subsidized plan (including through a spouse's job), you may be able to deduct what you pay for health insurance. The IRS calculates it on Form 7206, the self-employed health insurance deduction. It lowers your taxable income, which makes coverage cost less than the sticker price.

The rules have details, like the deduction can't be more than your business profit, so check with whoever does your taxes.

When your income is all over the place

Commission months, slow seasons, a big contract in December. Self-employed income is lumpy, and that matters if you get a Marketplace subsidy. Your subsidy is based on what you estimate you'll earn. If you end up earning more, you may have to pay some of it back when you file taxes.

Say you estimated $40,000, then landed two big contracts and finished at $65,000. Your subsidy was sized for $40,000, and the difference can come due in April. Two ways to handle it: update your Marketplace income estimate when things change, or, if you consistently earn too much for meaningful help, compare a private plan, which doesn't depend on income at all.

Three mistakes I see all the time

  • Going without coverage because you're healthy. One ER visit or a broken bone can cost more than years of premiums.
  • Plans that aren't insurance. Health sharing ministries and some discount programs aren't health insurance and don't guarantee payment. Read what you're buying.
  • Picking on premium alone. Check the deductible, out-of-pocket max, and whether your doctors are in the network.

The bottom line

Your income decides most of this. If it's low enough for a real Marketplace subsidy, that's usually the deal. If you earn too much for help and you're healthy, a private plan priced on your age is worth pricing before you pay full Marketplace rates. Either way, if you show a profit, claim the deduction. And don't go bare because you feel fine. That's the one move that can actually wreck a young business.

Questions I get

Can I stay on my parent's plan if I'm self-employed?+

Yes, if you're under 26. Being self-employed doesn't change that. Coverage on a parent's plan generally lasts until 26; on a Marketplace plan it runs through December 31 of the year you turn 26. Check the plan's rules, since some differ.

Is self-employed health insurance tax deductible?+

Often, yes. If you have a net profit from self-employment and you aren't eligible for an employer-subsidized plan (including through a spouse), you may be able to deduct your premiums using IRS Form 7206. Confirm with your tax preparer.

What if my income changes a lot during the year?+

If you get a Marketplace subsidy, it's based on your estimated income. If you earn more than you estimated, you may have to pay some back at tax time, so update your estimate when your income changes. Private plans don't depend on income at all.

Can I buy a private plan any time?+

Usually, yes. Private medically underwritten plans can typically start in any month. Marketplace plans need open enrollment or a qualifying life event, like losing other coverage.
