Self-employed in your 20s or 30s: your health insurance options

The first year working for yourself is when most people realize nobody is handing them a health plan anymore. Here are your three options, which one usually wins, and a tax deduction worth asking your tax preparer about.

By Josh Cutlan · independent agent, NPN 22078996
Updated October 21, 2026 · 5-min read
In short

You have three main options: stay on a parent's plan until 26, buy a Marketplace plan, or buy a private plan priced on your age and health. With income low enough for a Marketplace credit, that's often hard to beat. Above the credit's ceiling and healthy? A private PPO is usually worth pricing. And if you show a profit, the IRS may let you deduct the premiums.

1. A parent's plan (until 26)

If you're under 26, you can usually stay on a parent's plan even if you're self-employed, married, or out of school. If the parent's plan came from the Marketplace, your coverage runs until the year of your 26th birthday ends. A parent's job-based plan generally covers you until 26, but each plan sets its own cutoff, so ask.

It often costs you the least, but not always for your parent. Adding you can bump their cost up, and you may not have the doctors you want where you live.

2. A Marketplace plan

Marketplace plans accept every applicant and cover conditions you already have. What you pay depends on your age, where you live, and your income. With a premium tax credit in play, a Marketplace plan can come in cheaper than anything else. Earn more and the credit shrinks or disappears, leaving you at full price.

Enrollment happens inside the open enrollment window (for 2027 plans, from November 1, 2026) or within a special enrollment period after a life event, like losing other coverage. Many Marketplace plans are HMOs or EPOs with local networks.

3. A private plan

Private plans ask health questions and price you on your age and health. For a healthy 20- or 30-something, that usually works in your favor, and many come with PPO networks, so specialists don't need a referral. They can usually start the first of any month.

The trade-off is real: not everyone is approved, and the coverage list is the plan's own. I go through exactly what's covered before you apply.

Turning 26 soon? Don't let the birthday sneak up on you

  1. Find your end date. Ask your parent's plan when your coverage stops. It might be your birthday, the end of that month, or the end of the year.
  2. Mark your window. Losing the parent's plan unlocks a Marketplace special enrollment period. A private plan doesn't need one, so if you're approved, you can set the start date for the day after your old plan ends.
  3. Compare both before you pick. Your income decides whether the Marketplace is the deal or a private plan is.

The costliest mistake: waiting until the coverage has already ended. Even a few uninsured weeks is a risk you don't need to take.

The tax break many 1099 workers miss

With a net profit from your business and no access to an employer-subsidized plan (including through a spouse's job), the IRS may let you deduct what you pay for health insurance. The IRS calculates it on Form 7206, the self-employed health insurance deduction. It lowers your taxable income, so your coverage costs you less than the premium alone suggests.

The rules have details, like the deduction can't be more than your business profit, so run it past your tax preparer; this isn't tax advice.

When your income arrives in bursts

Commission months, slow seasons, a big contract in December. Income that comes in bursts matters if you get a Marketplace credit, because the credit is sized to what you estimate you'll earn. End the year higher than the estimate and you repay the extra when you file, with no cap starting in 2026.

Say you estimated $40,000, then landed two big contracts and finished at $65,000. Your credit was sized for $40,000, and the difference can come due in April. Two ways to handle it: update your Marketplace income estimate when things change, or, if you're above the credit's ceiling year after year, price a private plan, which doesn't depend on income at all. Three mistakes that cost 1099 workers the most

  • Going without coverage because you're healthy. One ER visit or a broken bone can cost more than years of premiums.
  • Plans that aren't insurance. Health sharing ministries and some discount programs aren't health insurance and don't guarantee payment. Read what you're buying.
  • Picking on premium alone. Check the deductible, the out-of-pocket max, and look for your doctors on the network list.

Where I land

Your income decides most of this. Low enough for a meaningful Marketplace credit, and that's usually the deal. Above the credit's ceiling and healthy, and a private plan priced on your age deserves a quote before you pay full Marketplace rates. Either way, if you show a profit, claim the deduction. And don't go without because you feel fine. That's the one move that can sink a young business.

What people ask me

Can I stay on my parent's plan if I'm self-employed?+

Yes, if you're under 26. Working for yourself doesn't change that. A parent's job-based plan generally covers you until 26; if the parent's plan came from the Marketplace, your coverage runs until the year of your 26th birthday ends. Check the plan's rules, since some differ.

Does the IRS let me deduct my premiums?+

Often, yes. With a net profit from self-employment and no access to an employer-subsidized plan (including through a spouse), the IRS may let you deduct your premiums using Form 7206. Run it past your tax preparer.

My income jumps around. What happens to my subsidy?+

A Marketplace subsidy is sized to the income you estimated. End the year above that number and you may have to pay some back at tax time, so update your estimate when your income changes. Private plans don't depend on income at all.

Can I buy a private plan any time?+

Usually, yes. A private plan can typically start the first of any month. A Marketplace plan waits for the enrollment window, or for a qualifying life event such as losing other coverage.

Do you help if I'm not in Florida?+

Yes. My license covers 19 states, with Nevada, Iowa, and Montana coming soon. Check the list on my homepage, and if I can't help in your state, I'll point you the right way.
